01

Before we start

This story starts where Act 1 ended. The Telco Revamp established a trust-first payment model — one that proved users would pay digitally when the experience made them feel confident enough to do so.

Act 2 asked a different question: Could the same model scale across every bill a user manages?

"Could the same model scale across every bill a user manages?"

02

The gap

Multiple bills. Multiple systems. No single view.

Users were managing mobile services, home broadband, and fixed-line accounts — each in a different place, each with a different flow. Electricity, water, and ROP fines sat in silos that most customers didn't know existed inside the app.

The problem wasn't discovery alone. Each service had its own login logic, its own confirmation pattern, its own language for what "paid" meant. Users weren't failing to pay — they were failing to feel in control of what they owed.

We weren't solving a payment problem.
We were solving a mental model problem.
03

The principle

"People shouldn't have to understand categories, bill types or provider relationships. They should know exactly what they owe, what they're paying, and whether it worked. Everything else is the platform's problem."

Design principle · Act 2

One view. One total. One decision.

The principle forced every design decision through the same filter: does this reduce cognitive load, or does it add to it? If a user ever had to think about which service they were paying — rather than simply paying — the design had failed.

04

My role beyond the screens

The design challenge wasn't technical — it was organisational. Electricity, water, and ROP each came with different constraints, different internal priorities, and different technical realities. Every provider had their own API contract, their own edge cases, their own definition of "success."

My role was reframing service-specific experiences into a single customer outcome. That meant working across product, engineering, and business stakeholders to establish a shared language — and holding the design system steady while everything around it shifted.

Cross-functional alignment Systems design Stakeholder facilitation Design strategy
05

The question

"What would this feel like if it were crystal-clear and reassuring?"

Every screen decision was stress-tested against this. Not: does it work? But: does it feel safe? Would someone pay with confidence, or would they pause, second-guess, and call the contact centre?

All-in-one bill payment rarely fails because of vision. It fails because complexity accumulates quietly over time.

06

What made this hard

Different rules. Different workflows. Different constraints.

Each provider brought its own definition of a payment cycle. Electricity bills issued on a monthly cadence. Water bills on a different cycle. ROP fines had no cadence at all — they appeared when they appeared.

The technical constraints compounded the UX complexity. Some providers required manual account reference entry. Others had no real-time confirmation mechanism. One returned a status code that could mean success or pending — the platform had to decide which.

The design had to be robust enough to handle every variation without surfacing that complexity to the user. The unified interface was the product of significant constraint negotiation, not a clean slate.

07

What's next

Expansion without complexity was the design mandate going forward. Adding new services was never the challenge — the challenge was adding them without each new integration adding friction to everything that already worked.

The unified model created the conditions for that. Every new service now inherits the same payment language, the same confirmation pattern, the same success state. The system scales. The user experience doesn't change.

The system that's hardest to build is the one that feels easiest to use.
